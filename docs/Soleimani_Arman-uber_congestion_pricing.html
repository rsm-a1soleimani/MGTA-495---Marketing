A/B Testing Uber Pricing in NYC’s Congestion Zone

How to measure what riders will actually tolerate

Author

Arman Soleimani

Published

Invalid Date

The Setup

In January 2025, NYC started charging tolls on every vehicle entering Manhattan below 60th Street. For Uber, that meant a $1.50 congestion surcharge per trip — passed directly to riders.

What changed

A forced price increase — every Uber trip into lower Manhattan suddenly cost $1.50 more, overnight.

What we observed

Despite higher prices, trip volume in the congestion zone stayed high. Riders kept riding.

The opportunity

If riders absorbed a $1.50 surcharge without flinching — could Uber raise base fares further?

Why Not Just Look at What Happened?

The congestion policy gave us useful evidence — but observational data has limits. To safely change prices further, you need a controlled test.

Approach What you learn Good enough to act on?
Before/after trip data Demand held up after the surcharge Maybe — confounded by season, events
Congestion zone vs. outer boroughs Zone-specific price effect Better — but zones differ in other ways
Randomized A/B test Exactly how this price affects these riders Yes — cleanest signal for a decision

Observational data tells you what happened. An A/B test tells you what caused it — and what will happen if you do it again, intentionally.

What Is A/B Testing?

Split your users randomly into two groups. Give each a different experience. Measure what changes. Decide based on the difference.

Group A — Control

  • Standard Uber fares
  • No change from today
  • Sets the baseline to compare against

Group B — Treatment

  • Slightly higher base fare in the congestion zone
  • Everything else identical
  • Reveals how riders respond

Because assignment is random, any difference in behavior between the two groups can only be explained by the price change — not by who the riders are or when they travel.

Designing the Test

Three decisions matter before you start.

01 — What to test A 5% base fare increase on trips starting in the Manhattan congestion zone. Small enough to limit downside; large enough to detect a real signal.

02 — How to split users Randomly assign by user ID — not by time or location. This prevents the same person from seeing different prices on different days, which would confuse the measurement.

03 — How long to run it At least 4–6 weeks. Short tests get distorted by day-of-week patterns and weather. Long enough for riders to adapt their behavior — not just react to novelty.

What to Measure

Not all metrics are equal. You need a primary metric that defines success, and guardrail metrics that flag if something is breaking.

Type Metric Why it matters
Primary Revenue per rider Are we actually making more?
Primary Trip completion rate Are riders finishing rides at the same rate?
Guardrail Cancellation rate Price shock visible at checkout?
Guardrail 30-day retention Are riders quietly leaving over time?
Competitive Lyft usage in zone Are riders switching platforms, not just stopping?

What the Congestion Data Already Tells Us

The January 2025 surcharge was, in effect, a real-world price test — one we can use to set expectations for the A/B test.

Measured price elasticity: ε = −0.135

A 1% price increase → 0.135% fewer trips. Demand is highly inelastic.

Compare to airline tickets (≈ −1.2) where a 1% increase causes a 1.2% drop. Manhattan Uber is far stickier.

What this means for the A/B test:

  • We expect a 5% fare increase to reduce trips by roughly 0.7%
  • Revenue should increase for Group B
  • If we see a much bigger drop, something has changed — the A/B test catches that before we commit

Reading the Results

After 4–6 weeks, two things need to be true before you act.

Is the difference real? Statistical significance

Did we collect enough data that the result isn’t just noise? With millions of trips per month, this threshold is reached quickly.

Standard threshold: p < 0.05

Does the difference matter? Practical significance

A real result might still be too small to act on. A 0.01% revenue increase isn’t worth the operational complexity of changing the pricing model.

Set a minimum threshold before the test: e.g., at least 3% revenue uplift

Both boxes need to be checked. Statistical significance without practical significance is just noise that survived the math.

Making the Call

At the end of the test, you’re in one of three situations.

Ship it — Revenue up, trip volume held, guardrail metrics clean. Roll the increase out to all riders in the congestion zone.

Iterate — Revenue up but cancellations also rose. Try a smaller increase (3%) or test different framing — “congestion fee” vs. a silent base fare change.

Don’t ship — Trip volume dropped more than expected, or retention fell. Elasticity in practice is worse than the model predicted. Hold prices flat and revisit.

The whole point of the test: a “don’t ship” result costs almost nothing — only a small share of riders saw the higher price, for a few weeks, with no permanent change.

Key Takeaways

01 — Congestion pricing created a natural signal, but not a safe enough one to act on alone. An A/B test confirms whether the finding holds when Uber intentionally raises prices.

02 — A/B testing turns a risky pricing decision into a measured one. You expose a small group, measure the real impact, and then decide — not the other way around.

03 — Watch the guardrail metrics, not just the headline number. Revenue can rise in the short run while retention quietly deteriorates.

04 — The value of the test is the option to stop. If results are worse than expected, you’ve learned something important at minimal cost — before committing to a change affecting millions of trips a month.
