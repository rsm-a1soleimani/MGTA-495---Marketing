The Setup

In January 2025, NYC started charging tolls on every vehicle entering Manhattan below 60th Street. For Uber, that meant a $1.50 congestion surcharge per trip — passed directly to riders.

What changed

A forced price increase

Every Uber trip into lower Manhattan suddenly cost riders $1.50 more — overnight, with no opt-out.

What we observed

Demand barely moved

Despite the higher price, trip volume in the congestion zone stayed high. Riders kept riding.

The opportunity

Room to price higher?

If riders absorbed a $1.50 surcharge without flinching, could Uber raise base fares further — and by how much?

Why Not Just Look at What Happened?

The congestion policy gave us useful evidence — but observational data has limits. To safely change prices further, you need a controlled test.

ApproachWhat you learnGood enough to act on?
Look at before/after trip data Demand held up after the surcharge Maybe — confounded by season, economy, events
Compare congestion zone vs outer boroughs Zone-specific effect of the price change Better — but zones differ in other ways too
Randomized A/B test Exactly how this price change affects these riders Yes — cleanest possible signal for a decision

Observational data tells you what happened. An A/B test tells you what caused it — and what will happen if you do it again, intentionally.

What Is A/B Testing?

Split your users randomly into two groups. Give each group a different experience. Measure what changes. Decide based on the difference.

Group A — Control

Current experience

  • Standard Uber fares
  • No change from today
  • Sets the baseline to compare against
Group B — Treatment

New experience

  • Slightly higher base fare in the congestion zone
  • Everything else identical
  • Reveals how riders respond

Because assignment is random, any difference in behavior between the two groups can only be explained by the price change — not by who the riders are or when they travel.

Designing the Test

Three decisions matter before you start — get these wrong and the results won't be usable.

01

What to test

A 5% base fare increase on trips starting in the Manhattan congestion zone. Small enough to limit downside if it backfires, large enough to detect a real signal in the data.

02

How to split users

Randomly assign riders by user ID — not by time of day or location. This prevents the same person from seeing different prices on different days, which would confuse the measurement.

03

How long to run it

At least 4–6 weeks. Short tests get distorted by day-of-week patterns, weather events, and randomness. Long enough for riders to adapt their behavior — not just react to novelty.

What to Measure

Not all metrics are equal. You need a primary metric that defines success, and guardrail metrics that flag if something is breaking.

Primary metric

Revenue per rider

Does Group B generate more revenue per active user? This is the main thing we want to increase.

Primary metric

Trip completion rate

Are Group B riders completing trips at the same rate? A drop here signals price resistance kicking in.

Guardrail metric

Cancellation rate

If Group B cancels more after seeing the fare, the price is too visible — even if trips still complete.

Guardrail metric

Rider retention (30-day)

Are Group B riders returning less over time? Short-run inelasticity can mask long-run churn.

Also worth tracking: Lyft usage in the zone. If Group B riders switch platforms rather than stop riding entirely, that's a different problem than dropping out altogether.

What the Congestion Policy Already Tells Us

The January 2025 surcharge was, in effect, a real-world price test — one we can use to set expectations for the A/B test.

−0.135
Measured price elasticity in the congestion zone

A 1% price increase → 0.135% fewer trips. Demand is highly inelastic — riders barely adjusted when the surcharge hit.

Compare to airline tickets (≈ −1.2) where a 1% increase causes a 1.2% drop in bookings. Manhattan Uber is far stickier than that.

What this means for the A/B test

We have a prior, not a guess

We expect a 5% fare increase to reduce trips by roughly 0.7%. That means:

  • Revenue should increase for Group B
  • Trip volume drop should be small and detectable
  • If we see a much bigger drop, something has changed — the A/B test tells us that before we commit

Reading the Results

After 4–6 weeks, you compare Group A and Group B on every metric. Two things need to be true before you act.

Is the difference real?

Statistical significance

Did we collect enough data that the difference between groups isn't just noise? With millions of Uber trips per month in Manhattan, this threshold is typically reached quickly.

Standard threshold: p < 0.05 — meaning less than a 5% chance the result is a fluke.

Does the difference matter?

Practical significance

Even a statistically real result might be too small to care about. A revenue increase of 0.01% isn't worth the operational complexity of changing the pricing model.

Uber would set a minimum threshold — e.g., "at least 3% revenue uplift" — before shipping.

Both boxes need to be checked. A result can be statistically significant without being practically meaningful — and a big effect that isn't statistically reliable is just noise.

Making the Call

At the end of the test, you're in one of three situations.

✓

Ship it

Revenue up, trip volume held, guardrail metrics clean. Roll the price increase out to all riders in the congestion zone.

↻

Iterate

Revenue up but cancellations also up. Try a smaller increase (3%) or test different framing — e.g., "congestion fee" vs. a silent base fare change.

✗

Don't ship

Trip volume dropped more than expected, or 30-day retention fell. The elasticity in practice is worse than the model predicted. Hold prices flat for now.

The whole point of the test is that a "don't ship" result costs almost nothing — only a small share of riders saw the higher price, for a few weeks, with no permanent change.

Key Takeaways

01

Congestion pricing created a natural signal — but not a safe enough one to act on alone

The surcharge data suggests riders are price-insensitive in Manhattan. An A/B test confirms whether that holds when Uber intentionally raises prices further.

02

A/B testing turns a risky pricing decision into a measured one

Rather than changing prices for all riders and hoping the analysis was right, you expose a small group, measure the real impact, and then decide.

03

Watch the guardrail metrics, not just the headline number

Revenue can go up in the short run while retention quietly deteriorates. A test that only checks one metric can miss the actual problem.

04

The value of the test is the option to stop

If results are worse than expected, you've learned something important at minimal cost — before committing to a change that affects millions of trips a month.
